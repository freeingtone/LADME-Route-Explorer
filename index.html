    After entry: three processes can overlap

    Distribution

    Medicines are carried by the blood to tissues. They do not travel directly to a target organ.

    Some drug can also return from tissues to blood.

    Metabolism

    The liver is a major site of metabolism, not the only one.

    Metabolism chemically changes a drug into metabolites. These can circulate in blood, enter tissues, and later be excreted; some remain active.

    Excretion

    Kidneys filter blood and form urine. Bladder stores urine.

    Some drugs and metabolites leave in urine; other routes of removal also exist.

    Can fitting and acting be different?

    Start with the meaning. Predict, explore, then learn the name.

    1 · Can two molecules fit the same place but do different things?

    Imagine a key that fits a lock. Fitting is only the first part: can it turn and change what happens?

    A cell has proteins that receive signals. These are called receptors. A drug may fit one of these proteins, but fitting alone does not tell us what it will do.

    Think of three keys: one turns fully, one turns only part-way, and one fits but cannot turn. We will explore each before naming it.

    Analogy limit: real receptors are not rigid locks; they change shape, and cells can change how they respond. Not every drug acts through a receptor.

    2 · Fit, then switch on

    First try a key that turns fully. More turning keys can produce a bigger response, until the system approaches its limit. Then compare a key that turns only part-way.

    “Response” means the change we measure in the cell or body. “Concentration” means how much of a substance is present in a given volume. Here, all numbers are invented teaching units.

    Now name what you observed: potency and efficacy

    Potency describes how much concentration is needed for a specified response. Competition means more is needed here.

    Efficacy describes the ability to produce a response; in this comparison, the partial agonist has a lower maximum. Greater potency does not mean a greater maximum or greater safety.

    5 · Connect the meaning to medicines

    Salbutamol activates receptors on airway smooth muscle and helps it relax: an example of agonist action. A model, not a prescribing guide.

    Antihistamines block histamine’s effects at histamine receptors. A model, not a prescribing guide; their detailed action is explained in Teacher notes.

    Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors, reducing adenosine’s action there. A model, not a prescribing guide.

    Connect the two big ideas: drug journey and drug effect

    Pharmacokinetics means what the body does to a drug: how it enters, moves, changes and leaves.

    Pharmacodynamics means what a drug does to the body: how it changes a response. Blood carries medicines to tissues; medicines do not travel directly to a chosen organ.

    Advanced · equations and assumptions

    At equilibrium: D = 1 + A/Kd + B/Ki. Agonist occupancy = (A/Kd)/D; antagonist occupancy = (B/Ki)/D; free fraction = 1/D.

    Response (%) = 100 × ε × agonist occupancy. ε = 1 for full agonist or 0.4 for partial agonist; Ki = 50 concentration units. EC50 = Kd × (1 + B/Ki), defined relative to that agonist’s own maximum.

    This model assumes one binding site, reversible competition, zero basal activity, no spare receptors, and response proportional to occupancy. Kd and EC50 need not be equal in real systems. Noncompetitive antagonism, inverse agonism, full/partial agonist mixtures, signaling delays and desensitization are not modelled.

    Teacher notes · 45-minute lesson

    0–5 min: discuss the opening question. 5–15 min: full and partial activation. 15–25 min: binding without activation. 25–35 min: competition and recovery. 35–45 min: medicine examples, reflection and review.

    Misconceptions: fitting means activating; an antagonist must cause an opposite response; a partial agonist becomes full if enough is added; potency means safety; blood delivers a medicine only to a chosen organ.

    Prediction answers: 1 response approaches a limit; 2 the partial maximum stays lower; 3 no activation without agonist; 4 competing blocker reduces response at the same agonist concentration; 5 more agonist can overcome reversible competition. Each follows from binding and activation being different.

    H1 antihistamines are generally inverse agonists: they favour the inactive receptor state. “Block histamine’s effects” is suitable beginner wording, but they are not exact examples of this zero-basal-activity neutral antagonist model.

    Not modelled: noncompetitive antagonism, inverse agonism, desensitisation, agonist mixtures, spare receptors, signaling delays or clinical toxicity. No dose advice or decisions about starting/stopping medicines are provided.

    Written reflection is self-assessed against a model explanation; the app does not judge free text. Answers stay on this page during the session and are not sent anywhere.

    Fitting is not the same as acting

    Drug journey: blood carries medicines to tissues. Pharmacokinetics describes entry, movement, change and removal. Drug effect: pharmacodynamics describes how a drug changes a response. Not every medicine acts through receptors.

    Three key meanings

    Agonist: fits and activates. Partial agonist: activates with a lower maximum in the same system. Competitive antagonist: fits without activating and competes for the same place.

    Key-and-lock analogy: a key can turn fully, partly, or fit without turning. Real receptors are not rigid locks.

    What the experiments teach

    Binding alone is not activation. More agonist can overcome reversible competition in this model. More partial agonist cannot reach the full maximum. Potency means how much is needed for an effect; efficacy concerns the ability to produce a response.

    Explain why

    More drug is not always better: responses can approach a limit while unwanted effects may increase. Medicines are carried in blood to tissues, not directly to a chosen organ.

    How much reaches the blood?

    Bioavailability (F) is the fraction of a dose that reaches circulating blood as unchanged drug.

    of each dose will eventually reach blood
    has entered blood so far

    “Entered so far” counts everything that has reached the blood. Some of it may already have moved into tissues or left the body. “In blood now” shows what remains there at this moment. Some slow-release drug may enter after the graph ends.

    Try a bioavailability calculation with AUC

    AUC means area under a concentration–time curve: a measure of total exposure. To estimate absolute oral bioavailability, compare oral and IV doses of the same compound.

    F (%) = (AUC oral ÷ AUC IV) × (Dose IV ÷ Dose oral) × 100

    Use total AUC from time zero to infinity, matching concentration and time units, and matching dose units. This comparison assumes linear kinetics and the same clearance for both routes. The graph now reports amount AUC₀–₂₄ and its matched IV ratio. With the same constant blood volume these ratios also apply to concentration AUC, but this standalone exercise uses total concentration AUC to infinity.

    With repeated dosing, the liberation graph shows one dose; the body diagram and main graph combine all doses given so far. Disposition half-life stays fixed in this linear model.

    Explore release from an oral solid separately from absorption into blood. These are teaching models, not fitted dissolution profiles.

    1. Drug liberation

    First-order uses the Tablet release slider above. Zero-order uses the rate below. Both can have a delay before release begins.

    Release is capped at 100%. Released drug may still be in the gut: release does not mean absorption. A delay is an idealised formulation lag, not a stomach-emptying or enteric-coating prediction.

    2. Half-life in this model

    One-compartment reference:
    t½ = ln(2) / k = 0.693147 / k

    For the current two-compartment model:
    k₁₀ = kmetabolism + krenal
    S = k₁₀ + k₁₂ + k₂₁
    β = [S − √(S² − 4k₁₀k₂₁)] / 2
    Terminal disposition t½ = ln(2) / β

    k₁₂ is blood → tissue; k₂₁ is tissue → blood. Rates are in h⁻¹ and half-lives in hours. Metabolism removes unchanged parent drug, even while metabolites remain in the body.

    This standalone calculator does not change the simulation. It assumes a single exponential decay with no ongoing input. A zero-order release model has a time to 50% release, not a constant half-life.

    Teacher notes & model assumptions

    LADME processes overlap; they are not five separate time windows. Clicking a letter highlights a concept without changing time. Use the scrubber to examine any moment. Activity is calculated from current model transfer rates. Distribution scoring uses the absolute net rate |k₁₂ × blood − k₂₁ × tissue|. The two distribution arrows retain their separate one-way rates, and an “Exchange continues” label shows ongoing exchange even when net movement is small. Excretion activity includes unchanged drug and metabolites; metabolism and excretion describe different transfers, not interchangeable measures of parent-drug clearance. Gold marks the largest rate; other rates above 0.01 units/h are active. These thresholds are display choices, not physiological cutoffs. Bands use each process’s own maximum; stage bars use the current largest rate. Particles indicate active routes and are decorative, not individual molecules or measured counts. Clicking a stage pauses automatic explanation-following; it does not pause time.

    The illustrative oral model assumes full eventual absorption, first-order or zero-order release (optionally delayed), and first-order absorption, a fixed first-pass fraction, reversible blood–tissue exchange, liver conversion to metabolites, and renal excretion of unchanged drug and metabolites. Metabolite values use starting-drug equivalents, so compartments sum to the amount administered so far. First-pass processing is an instantaneous split, not a storage compartment.

    Route models: Each oral dose adds 100 units to its own solid formulation. Each SC dose adds 100 units to a dissolved injection-site depot; absorption follows a first-order rate, with complete eventual uptake assumed and no gut/liver first pass. Each IV bolus adds 100 units to systemic blood at its scheduled time, with no release or absorption step. All routes share the same blood–tissue exchange and elimination equations. Real SC uptake can be incomplete, include lymphatic transport, and depend on formulation and local blood flow; those effects are omitted. SC is not necessarily slower than oral for every medicine.

    Real medicines differ from this hypothetical compound. The model combines disintegration and dissolution into a single release process. First-order liberation uses an analytical cumulative release curve; absorption and disposition use 0.01-hour Euler steps. The model omits stomach emptying, intestinal metabolism, unabsorbed drug in feces, biliary excretion, protein binding, active metabolites, and saturation. Liberation can occur in the stomach or intestine. Metabolism can create active or inactive products. Excretion is physical removal; metabolism is chemical change. The graph represents a hypothetical compound. Real medicine facts are provided separately. No clinical dose or patient is represented. Published half-lives appear only in the separate medicine reference panel. Tissue exchange affects the simulated blood decline.

    Parameter transparency: All release, absorption, first-pass, tissue-exchange, metabolic, and renal rate settings below are selected teaching assumptions for the hypothetical compound. Named medicine facts are separate from this simulation. All rate constants have illustrative units of h⁻¹. First-pass fractions are particularly uncertain and are not inferred from the total amount metabolised.
